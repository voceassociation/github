AI governance: from model intelligence to institutional authority

A model produces outputs. An operating AI system can read data, call tools, query memory, trigger workflows and act through permissions. Governance begins where capability becomes authority.

01

The system, not the model

Model evaluation remains necessary, but institutional risk emerges from the whole stack: data, prompts, retrieval, tools, connectors, identities, permissions, memory, routing and human escalation. The same model can be low-risk in an isolated drafting interface and high-impact when connected to payments, CRM, medical workflows, infrastructure or internal knowledge.

VOCE separates AI intelligence from AI authority. Capability answers “what can the model do?”. Governance asks “what may the system do, with which data, under whose identity, and who can stop it?”

02

Agents, tools, API, IAM and MCP

Agentic AI changes the control surface. A system may decompose goals, select tools and execute sequences that were once performed manually. That makes identity and access management, API scopes, tool allow-lists, secrets management, approval gates and revocation mechanisms governance objects, not merely technical details.

MCP and other tool-connection layers increase interoperability, but also expand the number of paths through which an AI system may reach data or actions. The relevant control is therefore not only model alignment. It is least privilege, explicit delegation, traceable execution and rapid revocation.

03

RAG and the lifecycle of institutional knowledge

Retrieval-augmented generation can make answers more grounded while creating a second knowledge lifecycle alongside the source system. A document can be corrected, archived or deleted while an embedding, cached chunk or index entry remains retrievable if synchronization is incomplete.

A serious RAG control plane tracks provenance, document version, synchronization time, deletion behaviour, revocation status, access rights and citations. VOCE’s rule is simple: a knowledge system must be governed from ingestion to withdrawal, not only at publication.

04

Multi-model orchestration and vendor control

The model is increasingly one component inside a routed system. Different models may be assigned to coding, search, reasoning, cybersecurity, translation or low-cost routine tasks. This can improve resilience and economics, but it also creates a policy problem: who decides which model receives which data, and under which contractual and jurisdictional conditions?

Vendor-neutral architecture is not the absence of vendors. It is the capacity to benchmark, switch, restrict and revoke them without rebuilding the institution around one provider.

05

Observability, security and AI FinOps

A governed AI system needs operational telemetry. Institutions should be able to reconstruct which model ran, which context it received, what tools were called, what action occurred, what it cost and whether a human intervened. Without this, post-incident review becomes inference.

AI FinOps belongs in the same control layer. Token, compute and agentic action budgets are economic permissions. Cost anomalies can also reveal runaway workflows, duplicated calls, prompt abuse or architectural inefficiency.

06

Board accountability, assurance and regulation

Governance must connect technical controls with institutional accountability. In Europe, enforcement powers under the AI Act have applied since 2 August 2026 for provisions already in force, while major high-risk obligations follow later application dates. GDPR, cybersecurity obligations and sector rules continue to shape deployment. VOCE therefore treats compliance as one layer of governance, not its final objective.

The board-level question is whether the organisation can demonstrate ownership, inventory, risk classification, human oversight, incident response, supplier controls, evidence of testing and the ability to suspend an AI capability without disabling the wider business.

Key governance questions

01Who is the AI system or agent acting for?
02Which identity and permissions does it use?
03Which APIs, tools and data sources may it access?
04Which data may each model receive, and under which jurisdiction?
05Where is human approval mandatory?
06Can a tool, model or supplier be revoked without disabling the wider system?
07Can the institution reconstruct an incident end-to-end?
08Who has authority to stop the system?

Vico, Don Carlo and the architecture of responsibility

Giambattista Vico condensed a demanding idea into “verum ipsum factum”: we know most deeply what we can account for as made. Applied to AI governance, the principle is severe. An institution cannot treat an agentic system as an unknowable oracle once that system can act in its name. Verdi’s Don Carlo offers another lens: authority distributed across court, church and conscience becomes dangerous when responsibility is everywhere and nowhere. VOCE uses philosophy and opera as counterpoints to technical analysis, not as decoration.
